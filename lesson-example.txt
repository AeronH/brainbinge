Distance and Displacement
The first concepts we'll discuss are distance and displacement. While often used interchangeably in everyday language, they have distinct meanings in physics.

Distance
When you hear the word distance, you likely think of how far something has traveled. It's the total path length covered by an object.

Example: The distance between two cities could be 100 miles.
Distance is the total difference between two points along the path taken.
Distance is a scalar quantity, meaning it only has magnitude (a numerical value and unit). It does not include direction.
Distance is always positive.
Displacement
Displacement is similar to distance, but it encompasses direction as well. Direction is crucial for displacement.

Displacement is defined as the difference between the final position and the initial position of an object.
Displacement is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction.
Displacement can be positive or negative.
If you travel east or north, displacement has a positive value.
If you travel west or south, displacement has a negative value.

Scalar vs. Vector Quantities
Understanding the difference between scalar and vector quantities is fundamental.

Scalar Quantity:

Has magnitude only.
Example: Distance (e.g., 200 miles), speed, mass, time, temperature.
Question: A car travels 200 miles. Is this distance or displacement? Answer: This is a description of distance because no direction was indicated. You don't know if the car went east, west, north, or south.

Vector Quantity:

Has both magnitude and direction.
Example: Displacement (e.g., 300 miles north), velocity, acceleration, force, momentum.
Question: A car traveled 300 miles north. Is this distance or displacement? Answer: This describes displacement because both the magnitude (300 miles) and the direction (north) are provided.

Summary of Distance and Displacement
Distance: Total path length, scalar (magnitude only), always positive.
Displacement: Change in position, vector (magnitude and direction), can be positive or negative.
Positive displacement indicates movement in the positive direction (e.g., east, north).
Negative displacement indicates movement in the negative direction (e.g., west, south).
Speed and Velocity
Now, let's explore speed and velocity, which both describe how fast something is moving, but with a critical difference related to direction.

Speed
Speed tells you how fast something is moving.

It measures the rate at which an object covers distance.
Example: A car traveling 30 meters per second means that every second, the car travels a distance of 30 meters.
After 1 second, it has traveled 30 meters.
After 2 seconds, it has traveled a total of 60 meters.
Speed is a scalar quantity; it only has magnitude.
Speed is always positive.
Velocity
Velocity tells you not only how fast something is moving, but also where it is going.

It measures the rate at which an object's displacement changes.
Velocity is a vector quantity; it has both magnitude and direction.
Velocity can be positive or negative.
Distinguishing Speed and Velocity: An Example
Scenario 1: A train is moving at 45 meters per second.

This describes the speed of the train because it tells you how fast it's moving, but not its direction.
Scenario 2: A train is moving at 30 meters per second going west.

This describes the velocity of the train because it includes both the magnitude (30 m/s) and the direction (west).
The speed is 30 m/s, and the direction is west. Combined, this gives us velocity.
Key Differences Between Speed and Velocity
Speed:

Tells you how fast something is moving.
Scalar quantity (magnitude only).
Always positive.
Mathematically, speed is the absolute value of velocity.
If velocity is -20 m/s, the speed is 20 m/s.
Velocity:

Tells you how fast something is moving and where it is going.
Vector quantity (magnitude and direction).
Can be positive or negative.
A negative velocity (e.g., -20 m/s) indicates movement in the negative direction (e.g., west or south).

A truck might go from 0 to 60 miles per hour in 30 seconds.
A sports car might go from 0 to 60 miles per hour in 5 seconds.

Newton's Three Laws of Motion
Sir Isaac Newton formulated three fundamental laws that describe how objects move and interact.

Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)
Definition: An object at rest will remain at rest, and an object in motion will continue in motion with the same speed and in the same direction, unless acted upon by a net force.
This law introduces the concept of inertia, which is an object's resistance to changes in its state of motion.
Examples:

Object at Rest: A box sitting on the floor will not move unless you push or pull it (apply a force). - A force is a push or pull action. - A tension force is a force transmitted through a rope or cable when it is pulled taut.
Object in Motion: Imagine a box sliding across a surface. - If there were no friction, the box would continue to slide forever. - However, on Earth, friction acts as an opposing force, slowing the box down and bringing it to a stop. Friction always opposes motion. - In outer space, where there is almost no friction, objects tend to move forever (e.g., planets orbiting the sun).
Air Hockey Puck: An air hockey puck slides easily across the table because the air cushion drastically reduces friction. This allows it to travel a long distance before stopping, illustrating Newton's first law.
Newton's Second Law of Motion
Definition: The net force acting on an object is equal to the mass of the object multiplied by its acceleration.
